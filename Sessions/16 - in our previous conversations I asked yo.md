### 4. [Mar 30, 2026, 10:17:32 PM CEST] Prompt
> **what do you mean by low-end cut on ts? It eqs frequencies to lower volume or they are really cut off and are simply not there?**

#### Gem Response
These are excellent questions that get straight to the physics of your tone. Understanding exactly what is happening to your audio signal is what separates players who just twist knobs from players who architect their sound.
Here is the technical breakdown of what is actually happening inside those circuits.

### 1. The TS808 "Low-End Cut" (High-Pass Filter)

To answer your question directly: **It does not completely remove them; it gradually lowers their volume.**
The Tube Screamer circuit is not a "brick wall" that stops all low frequencies dead in their tracks. Instead, it uses a Resistor-Capacitor (RC) network in its clipping stage to create what audio engineers call a **high-pass filter**.
The cutoff frequency ($f_{c}$) is determined by the specific resistor and capacitor values in the circuit, governed by the formula $f_{c} = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}$. In a standard TS808, this cutoff point is roughly 720Hz.

* **How it works:** Frequencies above 720Hz pass through normally. Frequencies *below* 720Hz are gradually reduced in volume (attenuated) at a rate of 6dB per octave.
* **What this means:** At 360Hz (one octave down), your signal is 6dB quieter. At 180Hz (two octaves down), it is 12dB quieter. The extreme low-end is still there, but it is heavily suppressed.
* **Why it's brilliant for your rig:** Low frequencies carry a lot of acoustic energy. If you send full-volume sub-bass into the high-gain preamp tubes of your Mesa Mark VII, those tubes choke on the energy, resulting in a "flubby" or "muddy" distortion. By rolling off that low-end *before* it hits the amp, the amplifier's distortion remains incredibly tight and articulate, perfect for complex Dream Theater riffs on your Majesty.

### 2. Symmetrical vs. Asymmetrical Clipping

To understand this, you need to visualize your guitar signal as a smooth, rolling wave (a sine wave). "Clipping" happens when you push that wave so hard that it hits the voltage ceiling of the pedal's circuit. Because the wave cannot go any higher, the smooth peaks and valleys get chopped off, or "flattened." That flattening is what creates the sound of distortion.
Here is how the two pedals handle that chopping differently:
**Symmetrical Clipping (Your TS808)**

* **The Visual:** Imagine that smooth wave having both its top peak and its bottom valley chopped off at the exact same height. It is a perfectly symmetrical flattening.
* **The Sound:** This generates **odd-order harmonics**. It sounds very smooth, highly compressed, and extremely focused.
* **The Feel:** It creates a "liquid" feel under your fingers. It is highly predictable and smooths out the dynamics of your picking, which is why it is exceptional for fluid, fast legato runs (like Satriani) or tightening up heavily distorted modern metal tones.

**Asymmetrical Clipping (Your Boss SD-1)**

* **The Visual:** Imagine chopping the top of the wave off heavily, but only barely shaving the bottom of the wave. The Boss SD-1 achieves this by using three diodes in its clipping circuit (two on one side of the signal path, one on the other), creating an uneven ceiling.
* **The Sound:** This generates **even-order harmonics**. It sounds grittier, a bit more chaotic, and less compressed.
* **The Feel:** Because it is less compressed, it preserves the dynamics of your right hand much better. This uneven clipping is actually exactly how a real vacuum tube distorts when pushed to its limits. Therefore, the SD-1 sounds and feels much more like a natural, cranked amplifier. It is the secret to that biting, aggressive Iron Maiden or Deep Purple roar when pushed into your Marshall JVM.
