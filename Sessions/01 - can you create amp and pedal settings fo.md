#### Gem Response
It is a valid workaround, but you have to understand the physical limitations of what you are doing. You can certainly force your Majesty or Heafy to mimic a vintage garage-rock tone using coil-splits and extreme EQ, but it will always be a simulation.
Here is the honest breakdown of why relying on coil-splits and a Boss EQ pedal gets you 85% of the way there, and what is missing in that final 15%.

### The Pluses: Control and Frequency Manipulation

* **Gain Reduction:** Splitting the Illuminators on your Majesty or the pickups on your Epiphone cuts the output hitting the front of your JVM410c. This instantly reduces the natural compression, forcing the amp to react more dynamically to your right hand.
* **Surgical Lo-Fi:** A Boss GE-7 (or similar 7-band EQ) is incredibly powerful for this. Jack White’s vintage Airline guitars sound like they are being played through a broken AM radio. By radically band-passing your signal (cutting extreme lows and extreme highs while spiking the upper-mids), you can accurately replicate the specific frequency curve of cheap 1960s catalog guitars.
* **Out-of-Phase Honk:** Running the Majesty in the middle position with the coils split gives you a hollow, nasal, out-of-phase sound that is perfect for mimicking cheap vintage wiring.

### The Minuses: Physics and Dynamic Response

* **Sterile Transients:** Your guitars are built for high-fidelity precision. When you split a high-output ceramic or active pickup, you get a pristine, hi-fi single-coil sound. It lacks the inherent mechanical "slop," magnetic pull, and inductance of an overwound vintage P90 or a microphonic gold-foil pickup.
* **EQ Cannot Change Dynamics:** An EQ pedal alters amplitude at specific frequencies, but it does not change the compression or harmonic complexity of the pickup itself. A Boss EQ can make a Majesty sound *boxy*, but it cannot make it sound *unstable*. Vintage pickups microphonically feed back and inherently sag under heavy picking; modern precision pickups do not.

### How to Execute the Split + EQ Strategy

If you are using this method for *Frozen Charlotte* tracks, you need to use the EQ not to sweeten your tone, but to intentionally sabotage it.
**Guitar Configuration:**

* Use the **Music Man Majesty**.
* Select the middle pickup position.
* Engage the coil-split.
* Roll the guitar's tone knob down to about 6 to shave off the modern hi-fi treble.

**Boss EQ Settings (The "AM Radio" Filter):**
Place the Boss EQ *before* your overdrive pedals (Boss SD-1 / TS808) to choke the frequencies before they hit the clipping stage.

* **100Hz:** Cut to -10dB (Remove the modern, tight low-end).
* **200Hz:** Cut to -5dB.
* **400Hz:** Boost to +5dB (This adds the boxy, cheap-wood resonance).
* **800Hz:** Boost to +10dB (The absolute core of the vintage garage-rock bark).
* **1.6kHz:** Boost to +5dB.
* **3.2kHz:** Flat (0dB).
* **6.4kHz:** Cut to -10dB (Kill the hi-fi modern sizzle).

Push this highly mid-focused, bandwidth-limited signal into your Boss SD-1 and the Crunch channel of your Marshall JVM410c. It will sound appropriately choked, aggressive, and abrasive.

You are correct regarding the voltage, but you are overlooking the physics of the pickup itself.
Using the TC Spark Mini to compensate for the volume drop of a coil-split absolutely works for gain staging, but it will not fix the underlying tonal mismatch. You are confusing output level (amplitude) with the physical characteristics of the pickup (inductance, magnetic aperture, and frequency response).
Here is the exact breakdown of what happens when you hit that split coil with the Spark Mini.

### The Pluses: Gain Staging and Headroom

* **Gain Restoration:** Splitting a humbucker cuts its output roughly in half. The TC Spark Mini offers up to 20dB of clean boost. Kicking this on will instantly restore your signal level, allowing you to hit the front of the Boss SD-1 or the Marshall JVM410c with the same force as a full humbucker.
* **Preventing Anemia:** A split high-output ceramic pickup run straight into a mid-gain amp setting often sounds thin and anemic. The Spark prevents the signal from dying before it reaches the preamp tubes.

### The Minuses: Physics and Transparency

* **The Transparency Problem:** The Spark Mini is a highly linear, transparent FET boost. It does not color your tone; it simply amplifies exactly what you feed it. If you feed it the sterile, surgical, hi-fi sound of a split Illuminator or CrunchLab, the Spark will just give you a louder version of that exact same sterile sound. It will not add vintage warmth or garage-rock grit.
* **Magnetic Aperture:** A vintage P90 or cheap overwound single-coil has a wide magnetic window that reads a larger portion of the string, resulting in a slightly blurred, phase-smeared low end (the "slop"). A split modern humbucker reads a very narrow slice of the string, resulting in pristine clarity. A boost pedal cannot change how the magnet reads the string.
* **Inductance and Sag:** Vintage pickups physically compress and "sag" under heavy picking due to their specific Henry (inductance) ratings and coil capacitance. The Spark Mini is a solid-state circuit with massive headroom—it reacts instantly and does not sag.

### The Verdict on Your Routing

If you are determined to use the Majesty or Heafy to chase Jack White's tone, you *should* use the Spark Mini to make up for the split-coil volume drop, but you must place it strategically.
Run it like this: **Guitar (Split) -> TC Spark Mini -> Boss GE-7 EQ (AM Radio Filter) -> Boss SD-1 -> Marshall JVM410c.**
Use the Spark purely as a line driver to restore unity gain. Let the Boss EQ heavily band-pass the frequencies to fake the vintage character, and let the SD-1's asymmetrical clipping handle the aggressive breakup.
