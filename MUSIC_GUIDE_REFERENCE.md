Here is the technical breakdown of the HyperGravity's controls and an honest, real-world assessment of its place on your board.

## Control Breakdown

The HyperGravity is a digital compressor that gives you studio-style control. Here is exactly what each knob is manipulating in the algorithm:

* **Sustain:** This is a macro-control that simultaneously adjusts the threshold (the volume level where compression kicks in) and the ratio (how hard the signal is clamped down). Turning it up lowers the threshold and increases the ratio. High settings give you endless sustain but raise the noise floor drastically.
**Attack:** Controls the onset time—how quickly the compressor engages after you pick a string.

* *Counter-clockwise (Fast):* Clamps instantly. Good for chicken-picking or aggressively flattening funky rhythms, but it kills your pick transient.
* *Clockwise (Slow):* Allows the initial sharp transient of your pick to pass through uncompressed before the gain reduction starts. This is essential for maintaining the percussive "spank" of your Majesty's piezo or split-coil arpeggios.

* **Level:** Controls the overall makeup gain. Compression inherently reduces your volume by squashing peaks; this knob brings the unity volume back up. You can also use it to overdrive the front end of your TS808 or Mark VII by pushing it past unity.
* **Blend:** Controls the mix of your dry, uncompressed signal and the wet, compressed signal (known as parallel compression). Setting this at 50% allows your natural playing dynamics to dictate the attack, while the compressed signal sits underneath to artificially sustain the tails of the notes.
**Mode Toggle (Spectra / TonePrint / Vintage):**

* *Spectra:* The MD3 studio algorithm. It splits your signal into distinct low, mid, and high frequency bands and compresses them independently.
* *Vintage:* Emulates classic OTA analog stompboxes (like the MXR Dyna Comp). It deliberately squashes the whole signal, adding a colored, mid-heavy "thump."
* *TonePrint:* Loads custom presets beamed from your phone, allowing access to parametric EQs, specific crossover frequencies, and routing options hidden from the pedal's faceplate.

## Honest Review: The Pluses and Minuses

The HyperGravity is a highly capable digital tool, but it is not a traditional compressor. Here is the unvarnished analysis of how it performs.

| Pluses | Minuses |
| --- | --- |
| **True multiband compression** prevents heavy low-chugs from ducking the high strings. | **Digital AD/DA conversion** at the very front of your analog signal chain. |
| **Deep parameter editing** via TonePrint (crossovers, EQ, routing). | **Parameter paralysis**; the app is dense and not plug-and-play friendly. |
| **Blend knob** makes parallel compression easy and intuitive. | **Vintage mode falls short** of the warm, chewy feel of a true analog OTA circuit. |
| **High headroom** handles the Majesty's hot output without unwanted clipping. | **Digital noise floor** can become clinical or sterile at extreme Sustain settings. |

### The Verdict for Your Rig

For your specific goals—replicating the pristine, highly processed clean tones of John Petrucci, Tool, and Rush—**this is the right pedal.**
Standard analog stompbox compressors (like a Keeley or Dyna Comp) are fantastic for blues or country because they add a specific "color" and a spongy feel. #### 🎵 Query: *"I wnat to learn some 1/2 step downtuned songs, and some dagdad tuned songs, which guitar should i use for downtuning?"* `(Jul 21, 2026, 10:21:19 AM CEST)`
You are absolutely right to avoid alternating tunings on the Majesty. Adjusting a fully floating tremolo between E Standard, Eb Standard, and DADGAD throws the spring tension out of equilibrium, wrecking your bridge angle, action, and intonation. Keep the Majesty perfectly calibrated for E Standard.
Converting your Schecter Reaper into your dedicated downtuning workhorse is the most logical and practical solution.
Here is the technical breakdown of your plan to install a tremolo stopper (such as a Trem-ol-No or a brass block stopper) and run heavier strings on the Schecter:
**Pros of the Schecter Modification:**

* **Tuning Stability:** By installing a stopper and tightening the claw in the rear cavity, the tremolo block rests firmly against it. The bridge becomes "dive-only" (or fully fixed, depending on the hardware you choose). Changing to Eb or dropping to DADGAD will no longer throw the other strings out of tune.
* **Pickup Voicing:** The Petrucci Liquifire/Crunchlab set you installed in the Schecter is engineered precisely for lower tunings. The Crunchlab's ceramic bar magnet keeps the low-end incredibly tight and articulate when tracking dropped D or Eb power chords, which is essential for Tool or heavier Alice Cooper tracks.
* **Neck Profile:** The Reaper's neck radius and fretwork are highly conducive to the aggressive vibrato and bending you will use in lower tunings.

**Cons and Considerations:**

* **Loss of Upward Pitch:** You will completely lose the ability to pull up on the bar for flutters or upward pitch bends (unless you use a more complex locking system like the Trem-ol-No and frequently unlock it).
* **String Tension in DADGAD:** DADGAD requires dropping the 6th, 2nd, and 1st strings a full step. If you use a standard .010–.046 set, that low D string will lose significant tension, potentially causing fret buzz and a "flubby" attack under heavy picking.

**Expert Recommendation on String Gauge:**
Instead of a straight .010 or .011 set, I highly recommend a **"Skinny Top / Heavy Bottom" .010–.052 set**.

* The .010–.017 plain strings will feel normal in Eb Standard and comfortable for bends.

### 1. The TS808 "Low-End Cut" (High-Pass Filter)

To answer your question directly: **It does not completely remove them; it gradually lowers their volume.**
The Tube Screamer circuit is not a "brick wall" that stops all low frequencies dead in their tracks. Instead, it uses a Resistor-Capacitor (RC) network in its clipping stage to create what audio engineers call a **high-pass filter**.
The cutoff frequency ($f_{c}$) is determined by the specific resistor and capacitor values in the circuit, governed by the formula $f_{c} = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}$. In a standard TS808, this cutoff point is roughly 720Hz.

* **How it works:** Frequencies above 720Hz pass through normally. Frequencies *below* 720Hz are gradually reduced in volume (attenuated) at a rate of 6dB per octave.
* **What this means:** At 360Hz (one octave down), your signal is 6dB quieter. At 180Hz (two octaves down), it is 12dB quieter. The extreme low-end is still there, but it is heavily suppressed.
* **Why it's brilliant for your rig:** Low frequencies carry a lot of acoustic energy. If you send full-volume sub-bass into the high-gain preamp tubes of your Mesa Mark VII, those tubes choke on the energy, resulting in a "flubby" or "muddy" distortion. By rolling off that low-end *before* it hits the amp, the amplifier's distortion remains incredibly tight and articulate, perfect for complex Dream Theater riffs on your Majesty.

### 2. Symmetrical vs. Asymmetrical Clipping

To understand this, you need to visualize your guitar signal as a smooth, rolling wave (a sine wave). "Clipping" happens when you push that wave so hard that it hits the voltage ceiling of the pedal's circuit. Because the wave cannot go any higher, the smooth peaks and valleys get chopped off, or "flattened." That flattening is what creates the sound of distortion.
Here is how the two pedals handle that chopping differently:
**Symmetrical Clipping (Your TS808)**

* **The Visual:** Imagine that smooth wave having both its top peak and its bottom valley chopped off at the exact same height. It is a perfectly symmetrical flattening.
* **The Sound:** This generates **odd-order harmonics**. It sounds very smooth, highly compressed, and extremely focused.
* **The Feel:** It creates a "liquid" feel under your fingers. It is highly predictable and smooths out the dynamics of your picking, which is why it is exceptional for fluid, fast legato runs (like Satriani) or tightening up heavily distorted modern metal tones.

**Asymmetrical Clipping (Your Boss SD-1)**

* **The Visual:** Imagine chopping the top of the wave off heavily, but only barely shaving the bottom of the wave. The Boss SD-1 achieves this by using three diodes in its clipping circuit (two on one side of the signal path, one on the other), creating an uneven ceiling.
* **The Sound:** This generates **even-order harmonics**. It sounds grittier, a bit more chaotic, and less compressed.
* **The Feel:** Because it is less compressed, it preserves the dynamics of your right hand much better. This uneven clipping is actually exactly how a real vacuum tube distorts when pushed to its limits. Therefore, the SD-1 sounds and feels much more like a natural, cranked amplifier. In Parallel mode, your input signal is split into two identical paths. They pass through their respective EQ curves simultaneously and are then mixed back together at the output.

**Option B: The Line Out**

**Why:** Use this if you want to keep your FX Loop free for your delay/modulation pedals (Dreamscape/Flashback) *after* the preamp but *before* the Radar.

* *Note:* If you put your time-based effects (Delay/Reverb) *before* the Radar, they will sound "produced" (like a studio record). If you put them *after* the Radar, they will sound like they were added in post-production.

**⚠ Do not unplug your speaker cabinet yet!**

Since your signal is just the Preamp (Gain + EQ), the Radar needs to build the rest of the amp.

#### A. Power Amp Simulation: **ON (Crucial)**

Your signal currently has no "power tube" sound. You need to digitally add it back.

**Tube Selection:**

* **6L6:** Select this to match your Mark VII’s natural character. It will give you that tight low-end "thump" and glassy high end perfect for **Petrucci/Dream Theater**.

**High Pass Filter (HPF):** Set to roughly **80Hz - 100Hz**.

* **Daisy Chaining Warning:** Daisy-chaining analog drive pedals (TS808, SD-1) with digital modulation/delays (Dreamscape, Radar) causes ground loop hum and floating potential (feeling electric current on cable jacks).

* **18V Operation:** Only run pedals explicitly rated for 18V (like TC Spark) for extra clean headroom; DO NOT feed 18V to 9V-only digital pedals.
